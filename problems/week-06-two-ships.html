Two ships, one signal

dynamic programming strings

You are consulting for a group who monitor electronic signals from ships in coastal Atlantic waters. Two ships are each emitting a short sequence of 0s and 1s over and over, and the listeners want to be sure that what they hear is nothing more than those two emissions interleaved, with nothing extra added in.

Write xk for k copies of x run together. A string x′ is a repetition of x if it is a prefix of xk for some k; so 10110110110 is a repetition of 101. A string s is an interleaving of x and y if its symbols can be split into two subsequences, not necessarily contiguous, one a repetition of x and the other a repetition of y, with every symbol of s belonging to exactly one of them.

Here x and y are the two ships' repeating patterns and s is what you are hearing. Give an efficient algorithm that takes s, x and y and decides whether s is an interleaving of them.

Work along the signal one symbol at a time, handing each to whichever ship could have sent it. A ship will only take a symbol if that is genuinely the next one in its pattern. Nothing here unravels the signal for you.

Questions to sit with

  1. Unravel Example 1 completely. Now start over and find a different split of the same recording. How many are there, and does it matter which one you hand in?
  2. On Example 1 the habit "give it to X whenever X could have sent it" takes you all the way to the end. Try that same habit on Example 2. Where does it die, and what does that rule out for good?
  3. Example 2 does have a split, but neither greedy habit finds it, so you have to search. Before ruling anything out, how many ways are there of assigning its ten symbols to two ships?
  4. Undo back a few steps and come forward by different choices. Watch the "X has sent / Y has sent" line. Two different sets of choices can leave you in exactly the same position. Say precisely what that position consists of: what do you have to remember, and what can you throw away?
  5. Given your answer, how many genuinely different positions are there for a recording of length n? Write the recurrence that decides whether a position can be carried through to the end, and give its base case.
  6. Example 3 has no split at all, and you can still get as far as the final symbol before it collapses. Answering no means ruling out every route. How does your table manage that without walking all 211 of them?
  7. You never needed to record which symbols went to X, only how many. Why is the count enough on its own, and where exactly does the fact that x and y repeat get used?
  8. Someone hands you a finished split and says it works. How long does it take to check them? Compare that with deciding the question from scratch.
