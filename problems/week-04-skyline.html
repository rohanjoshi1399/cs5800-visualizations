Looking down from above

geometry divide and conquer

Hidden surface removal: when Woody stands in front of Buzz you should see Woody and not Buzz. Here is a clean geometric version of the same speed-up.

You are given n non-vertical lines in the plane, L1, …, Ln, with the ith given by y = aix + bi. Assume no three of them meet at a single point. Line Li is uppermost at x0 if its height there beats every other line's: aix0 + bi > ajx0 + bj for all j ≠ i. Line Li is visible if it is uppermost at some x-coordinate, meaning you could see a piece of it looking down from y = ∞.

Give an algorithm that takes the n lines and returns all the visible ones in O(n log n) time.

Slide the probe along and see which line is on top at that spot. Finding a line on top settles it for good; the lines you never catch up there are the interesting ones. Nothing here sorts anything for you.

Questions to sit with

  1. In Example 1, drag the probe from one end to the other and note every line you catch on top. One line never surfaces. Check whether any single other line sits above it everywhere — then explain how it can be invisible anyway.
  2. Example 1 has two parallel lines. Can both of them be visible? What does that settle about lines that share a slope, and how cheaply can you deal with them?
  3. Catching a line on top proves it is visible — you watched it happen. Can sliding the probe ever prove a line is not visible? What would a proof of invisibility have to look like?
  4. Slide from the far left to the far right and watch the slopes of the lines that take their turn on top. What order do they come in? Argue that it could not be any other order.
  5. Which line is on top when x is hugely negative, and which when x is hugely positive? You should be able to name both without looking at the picture.
  6. Example 3 has nine lines, and one of them is uppermost for only about a third of a unit of x. You may well have slid straight past it. What must an algorithm do so that it never misses a sliver like that?
  7. Someone hands you the visible lines, in left-to-right order, together with the x-coordinate where each takes over. How much work is it to check that they are right? Compare that with finding them yourself.
