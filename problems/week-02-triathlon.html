Mini-triathlon scheduling

greedy scheduling

Your friend is a camp counselor running a mini-triathlon: each contestant swims 20 laps, then bikes 10 miles, then runs 3 miles. Contestants are sent out in a staggered fashion, because the pool holds one person at a time. The first contestant swims, gets out, and starts biking; the moment the pool is free the second contestant starts swimming; and so on.

Each contestant has a projected swimming time, biking time, and running time. Once a contestant is out of the pool, nothing blocks them — any number of people can be biking and running at the same time.

The completion time of a schedule is the earliest moment at which every contestant has finished all three legs. Choose the order in which to send contestants out so the whole competition ends as early as possible, and give an efficient algorithm that produces such an order.

Questions to sit with

  1. Example 1 has three contestants, so there are only six lineups. Try all six. What is the best finishing time, and what do the good lineups have in common?
  2. Still in Example 1: does sending the fastest swimmer out first help? What about the slowest? Swimming time is the only thing anyone actually waits for, so why doesn't it settle the order?
  3. The pool is the one resource people queue for. Once a contestant climbs out of it, what can still delay the moment the whole competition ends?
  4. Example 2 has five contestants and 120 lineups, so trying them all is already unpleasant. If you were allowed to compute one number per contestant and sort by it, what number would you pick?
  5. Example 3 is strange: try several lineups and the finishing time never moves. Look at the times and work out why. What does that tell you about which numbers actually drive the answer?
  6. Take a lineup and swap two contestants who are next to each other. Exactly which contestants' finishing times can change, and which cannot? Use that to argue that a best lineup must have a particular shape.
  7. Can the competition ever end before the total of everyone's swimming times plus the smallest bike-plus-run total in the field? Explain why not, then check whether your best lineup in Example 2 hits that bound.
