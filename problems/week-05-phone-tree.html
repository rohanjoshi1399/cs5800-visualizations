The rained-out picnic

greedy trees

The spring ROTC picnic has fallen on a rainy day. The ranking officer must postpone it and notify everyone by phone. Everyone except the ranking officer reports to exactly one superior, so the hierarchy is a tree rooted at her, and each person can only ring their own direct subordinates.

Picture the process as rounds. In one round, each person who already knows can ring one of their direct subordinates. The ranking officer rings her subordinates one at a time; as soon as somebody hears, they start ringing their own subordinates one at a time; and so on until everyone knows.

How many rounds this takes depends on the order each person rings their subordinates in. Give an efficient algorithm that finds the minimum possible number of rounds and outputs a sequence of calls achieving it.

Pick an officer in the tree and reorder who they ring first. Every node shows the round in which that person hears the news. The model only plays out the order you set; it does not arrange anybody.

Click any officer with subordinates to work on their call order. The small number on each line is where that subordinate sits in their superior's queue; the number under a name is the round they hear in.

Questions to sit with

  1. In Example 1 the ranking officer has three subordinates. Find an order that finishes in five rounds, then one that takes seven. What exactly did you change, and why did it cost two rounds?
  2. Example 1 punishes you for ringing the longest chain of command first. Example 3 punishes you for it even harder — it is as bad as the worst order available. So "deal with the deepest branch first" is wrong. Why does depth mislead you?
  3. In Example 2, branch N contains six people and branch W contains seven, and yet N has to be rung first. More people under you is not it either. What number are you actually comparing when you decide who to ring first?
  4. Fix the order at the top, then go and change an order somewhere deep in a branch. Can that ever change what the top should have done? What does your answer let you do with the problem?
  5. Take any plan and swap two subordinates who are next to each other in one officer's queue. Whose round can change, and whose certainly cannot? Use that to argue what a best queue has to look like.
  6. You have seen this shape before. In week 2 the contestants shared one swimming pool, and you ordered them by how much work each had left after the pool. Here each officer's phone is the pool. What plays the part of "the work left over", and why is this the same argument?
  7. Someone hands you a complete plan and says it is optimal. How much work is it to compute how many rounds their plan takes? Is that the same as showing nothing beats it?
