Winter roads north

graphs shortest paths

Your friends are planning an expedition to a small town deep in the Canadian north. They have drawn up a directed graph whose nodes are intermediate destinations and whose edges are the roads between them.

Winter weather makes these roads slow, and how slow depends on when you travel. A travel web site will answer queries: given a road e = (v, w) and a proposed departure time t from v, it returns fe(t), the predicted arrival time at w. The site guarantees fe(t) ≥ t for every road and every time, so you cannot travel backward in time, and that fe is monotone increasing, so you never arrive earlier by setting out later. Beyond those two promises the functions can be anything.

They leave the start at time 0. Find the fastest way through the graph to the destination, with an algorithm that runs in polynomial time, counting one query to the web site as one computational step.

Drive a route yourself, one road at a time. Every road you take costs a query, and the web site answers only for the time you are actually standing there. Nothing here plans the trip for you.

Questions to sit with

  1. Example 1 has only two routes. Drive both. The first road of the winning route is not the road that gets you somewhere soonest, so what is wrong with the rule "always drive to whichever place you can reach earliest"?
  2. Still in Example 1: the last road takes 2 on one route and 20 on the other. Is that road fast or slow? A road's travel time is not a property of the road alone, so what is it a property of?
  3. You cannot ask the web site what a road costs without naming a departure time. That means you cannot label each edge with a number and then run a shortest-path algorithm you already know. What part of such an algorithm can you still keep, and what has to change?
  4. In Example 2 two different routes reach the destination at exactly the same moment, even though one of them got to an intermediate town a full unit earlier. Why didn't arriving earlier help? Could arriving somewhere earlier ever make your final arrival later?
  5. The site promises that fe is monotone increasing. Take that promise away: invent a road where setting out later gets you there earlier. Show how your method from question 3 now returns the wrong answer.
  6. In Example 3 the route with the fewest roads is not the fastest. Look at your query count after finding the best route by hand. How many queries should a good algorithm need, and in terms of what quantities?
  7. Someone hands you a route and claims it is the fastest. How many queries does it take to work out when that route arrives? Is that the same as confirming nothing beats it?
